# Fundamental Analysis

The words used to understand a US-listed public company through its official filings: what it earns, what it owns, what it decided, and what was happening around it. Terms are grouped in the order to learn them, and each group builds on the one before.

## Language

### 1. The company and its owners

**Public company**:
A company whose shares anyone can buy and sell, and which must therefore publish its finances.
_Example_: Apple is a public company. Mars, the chocolate maker, is family-owned and publishes nothing.
_Avoid_: Listed firm, issuer, corporation

**Share**:
One unit of ownership in a company.
_Example_: Apple has about 14.5 billion shares, so owning 145 shares means owning one hundred-millionth of the company.
_Avoid_: Stock, equity

**Ticker**:
The short code that identifies a company's shares where they are traded.
_Example_: Apple's ticker is AAPL and Costco's is COST.
_Avoid_: Symbol, stock code

**Share price**:
What one share last sold for.
_Example_: If the most recent trade of a Costco share was at $900, its share price is $900.
_Avoid_: Stock price, quote

**Market capitalization**:
The share price multiplied by the number of shares: what the market says the whole company's ownership is worth.
_Example_: Apple's market capitalization first ended a trading day above $3 trillion in June 2023.
_Avoid_: Market cap, market value, size

### 2. Time

**Fiscal year**:
The twelve-month period a company chooses for its annual reporting, which need not run January to December. Every yearly number in this glossary is a fiscal year number.
_Example_: Apple's fiscal year ends in late September, Microsoft's in June, and Walmart's in January.
_Avoid_: Financial year, calendar year, plain "year"

**Quarter**:
One of the four three-month periods inside a fiscal year.
_Example_: Apple's first quarter runs from the end of September to the end of December, so it contains the holiday shopping season.
_Avoid_: Period, Q

**Year-over-year change**:
How much a number moved compared with the same period one year earlier, as a percentage.
_Example_: Apple's revenue went from about $394 billion in fiscal 2022 to about $383 billion in fiscal 2023, a year-over-year change of about minus 3%.
_Avoid_: Progress, growth rate, YoY

### 3. The filings

**Filing**:
An official document a public company must submit to the US markets regulator, the SEC, where anyone can read it for free.
_Example_: Every Apple filing since the 1990s is available on the SEC's public database, EDGAR.
_Avoid_: Report, disclosure, document

**Annual report (10-K)**:
The filing that covers a full fiscal year: the checked financial statements, a description of the business, its risks, and management's explanation of the year.
_Example_: Apple filed its annual report for fiscal 2023 on 3 November 2023.
_Avoid_: Yearly report, annual filing

**Quarterly report (10-Q)**:
The shorter filing that covers one quarter. A company files three per year, because the annual report covers the fourth quarter.
_Example_: Apple's quarterly report for January to March arrives in early May.
_Avoid_: Quarterly filing, quarterlies

**Current report (8-K)**:
The filing a company must submit within four business days of something important happening.
_Example_: Disney filed a current report in November 2022 when Bob Iger returned as chief executive.
_Avoid_: Event filing, press release

**Proxy statement (DEF 14A)**:
The filing sent to shareholders before their yearly vote, showing how executives are paid and who sits on the board.
_Example_: Apple's proxy statement showed that Tim Cook's pay for 2022 was about $99 million.
_Avoid_: Proxy, pay report

**Earnings call**:
The phone conference held after each quarter where management presents the results and answers questions from analysts.
_Example_: Apple holds its earnings call about a month after each quarter ends.
_Avoid_: Conference call, results call

**Reported number**:
A figure calculated under the official US accounting rules, known as GAAP. Every number in this glossary is a reported number unless it says otherwise.
_Example_: Apple's net income of about $97 billion for fiscal 2023 is a reported number.
_Avoid_: GAAP number, official number, statutory number

**Adjusted number**:
A company's own version of a figure, leaving out costs that management considers one-off or not representative. Each company adjusts in its own way.
_Example_: For 2022 Uber showed an adjusted profit of about $1.7 billion while its reported net income was a loss of about $9 billion.
_Avoid_: Non-GAAP, core, underlying, normalized

**Restated number**:
A reported number for a past fiscal year that the company later published again with a different value. Screens show the latest restated number unless they say otherwise.
_Example_: A company changes how it counts revenue and, in the next annual report, publishes last year's revenue again under the new method.
_Avoid_: Revised, corrected, adjusted

**Guidance**:
Management's own public forecast for the coming quarter or year.
_Example_: In January 2019 Apple lowered its revenue guidance for the holiday quarter because of weak iPhone demand in China.
_Avoid_: Outlook, forecast, target

### 4. The income statement

**Income statement**:
The report showing what a company brought in, what it spent, and what was left over a period of time.
_Example_: Apple's income statement for fiscal 2023 covers 25 September 2022 to 30 September 2023.
_Avoid_: P&L, profit and loss, statement of operations

**Revenue**:
The money a company brings in from customers for its products and services, before any cost is taken out.
_Example_: Apple's revenue in fiscal 2023 was about $383 billion.
_Avoid_: Sales, net sales, top line, turnover

**Cost of revenue**:
The direct cost of making or delivering what was sold: parts, factory work, shipping, the servers behind a service.
_Example_: Apple's cost of revenue in fiscal 2023 was about $214 billion.
_Avoid_: Cost of goods sold, COGS, cost of sales, merchandise costs

**Gross profit**:
Revenue minus cost of revenue.
_Example_: Apple kept about $169 billion of gross profit from its $383 billion of revenue in fiscal 2023.
_Avoid_: Gross income, gross earnings

**Operating expenses**:
The costs of running the company that are not tied to each unit sold. In most filings these are research and development plus selling, general and administrative, and in some years other operating expenses as well.
_Example_: Apple's operating expenses in fiscal 2023 were about $55 billion.
_Avoid_: Opex, overhead, running costs

**Research and development**:
What a company spends creating new products and improving existing ones.
_Example_: Apple spent about $30 billion on research and development in fiscal 2023.
_Avoid_: R&D, product spend, engineering cost

**Selling, general and administrative**:
What a company spends on marketing, sales staff, management, offices, legal and accounting.
_Example_: Apple spent about $25 billion on selling, general and administrative in fiscal 2023.
_Avoid_: SG&A, overhead, admin

**Other operating expenses**:
Operating costs outside research and development and selling, general and administrative, such as writing down a business that lost value, restructuring, or the cost of opening new stores. The tool works it out as whatever lies between those two lines and operating income, so every year adds up.
_Example_: Microsoft's other operating expenses in fiscal 2015 were about $10 billion, mostly writing down the phone business it bought from Nokia.
_Avoid_: One-off charges, special items, non-recurring costs

**Operating income**:
Gross profit minus operating expenses: what the business itself earned, before interest and tax.
_Example_: Apple's operating income in fiscal 2023 was about $114 billion.
_Avoid_: Operating profit, operating earnings, EBIT

**Interest, other income and tax**:
Everything between operating income and net income: interest the company paid or earned, gains and losses outside the business itself, and income tax. The tool works it out as operating income minus net income.
_Example_: In fiscal 2023 Apple's operating income was about $114.3 billion and its net income about $97.0 billion. The $17.3 billion between them was mostly about $16.7 billion of income tax.
_Avoid_: Non-operating items, below the line, other expenses

**Income tax**:
The tax a company owes on its profit for the year. It is the largest part of what lies between operating income and net income for most companies.
_Example_: Apple's income tax in fiscal 2023 was about $16.7 billion.
_Avoid_: Tax provision, provision for income taxes, tax expense

**Interest and other income**:
Interest the company earned on its cash, minus interest it paid on its debt, plus gains and losses outside the business itself. It can add to net income or take from it. The tool works it out as net income minus operating income plus income tax.
_Example_: In fiscal 2023 Apple's interest and other income was about −$0.6 billion: it took a little from net income. Costco's was about +$0.6 billion in fiscal 2026, because it earned more interest on its cash than it paid.
_Avoid_: Non-operating income, other income (expense), net

**Net income**:
What is left for the owners after every cost, interest payment and tax.
_Example_: Apple's net income in fiscal 2023 was about $97 billion.
_Avoid_: Profit, net profit, earnings, bottom line

**Earnings per share**:
Net income divided by the number of shares.
_Example_: Apple's earnings per share for fiscal 2023 were $6.13, counting the shares already promised to employees as well as the existing ones.
_Avoid_: EPS, profit per share

### 5. The balance sheet

**Balance sheet**:
The report showing what a company owns and what it owes on one specific day.
_Example_: Apple's balance sheet for fiscal 2023 is dated 30 September 2023.
_Avoid_: Statement of financial position, the books

**Asset**:
Anything a company owns or is owed that has value.
_Example_: Apple's assets at the end of fiscal 2023 totalled about $353 billion.
_Avoid_: Holdings, resources

**Liability**:
Anything a company owes to someone else.
_Example_: Apple's liabilities at the end of fiscal 2023 totalled about $290 billion.
_Avoid_: Obligation, using "debt" for all liabilities

**Equity**:
Assets minus liabilities: what would be left for the owners if the company sold everything it owns and paid everything it owes.
_Example_: Apple's equity at the end of fiscal 2023 was about $62 billion, which is $353 billion of assets minus $290 billion of liabilities.
_Avoid_: Net worth, book value, using "equity" to mean shares

**Cash**:
Money in the bank plus very safe investments that can be turned into money within days.
_Example_: Apple held about $30 billion of cash at the end of fiscal 2023, plus far more in longer-dated investments.
_Avoid_: Liquidity, cash pile, war chest

**Restricted cash**:
Money a company holds but may use only for a set purpose, such as a deposit pledged to a lender. The balance sheet keeps it out of cash, though the cash flow statement adds it in.
_Example_: At the end of fiscal 2024 Tesla held about $0.9 billion of restricted cash, apart from its $16.1 billion of cash.
_Avoid_: Pledged cash, escrow, encumbered cash

**Receivables**:
Money customers owe for things the company has already delivered.
_Example_: When a supplier ships parts to a carmaker that has 60 days to pay, that amount sits in the supplier's receivables.
_Avoid_: Accounts receivable, AR, debtors

**Inventory**:
Goods a company holds that it has not yet sold, including materials and unfinished products.
_Example_: At the end of fiscal 2026 Costco carried about $19 billion of inventory on its shelves and in its depots.
_Avoid_: Stock, goods on hand

**Property, plant and equipment**:
The long-lived physical things a company owns and uses: land, buildings, machines, data centres.
_Example_: Walmart owns more than $100 billion of stores, land and distribution centres.
_Avoid_: PP&E, fixed assets, capital assets

**Goodwill**:
The amount a company paid for another company above the value of the things it actually received.
_Example_: When Microsoft bought LinkedIn for about $27 billion in 2016, about $17 billion of the price was recorded as goodwill.
_Avoid_: Brand value, premium, intangibles

**Debt**:
Money a company has borrowed and must repay with interest.
_Example_: Apple's debt at the end of fiscal 2023 was about $111 billion.
_Avoid_: Borrowings, leverage, using "debt" for all liabilities

### 6. The cash flow statement

**Cash flow statement**:
The report showing the actual money that came in and went out over a period, split into running the business, investing, and financing.
_Example_: Apple's cash flow statement shows where its $97 billion of net income ended up as real money in and out.
_Avoid_: Statement of cash flows, cash report

**Operating cash flow**:
The money that actually came in from running the business during a period.
_Example_: Apple's operating cash flow in fiscal 2023 was about $111 billion.
_Avoid_: Cash from operations, CFO, cash earnings

**Depreciation and amortization**:
The yearly slice of the cost of long-lived things bought in the past. It lowers net income even though no money leaves in that year.
_Example_: Apple recorded about $11.5 billion of depreciation and amortization in fiscal 2023.
_Avoid_: D&A, write-down, wear and tear

**Stock-based compensation**:
Pay given to employees in shares instead of money. It lowers net income but uses no cash.
_Example_: Apple paid about $11 billion of stock-based compensation in fiscal 2023.
_Avoid_: SBC, share-based pay, equity comp

**Capital expenditure**:
Money spent buying or building long-lived things such as factories, stores and data centres.
_Example_: Apple's capital expenditure in fiscal 2023 was about $11 billion.
_Avoid_: Capex, investment spending

**Free cash flow**:
Operating cash flow minus capital expenditure: the money the business produced that management is free to use.
_Example_: Apple's free cash flow in fiscal 2023 was about $100 billion.
_Avoid_: FCF, cash profit

### 7. Ways of reading the numbers

**Common-size analysis**:
Showing every line of the income statement as a percentage of revenue, to see where each dollar goes.
_Example_: Of every $100 Apple took in during fiscal 2023, about $56 went to cost of revenue, $14 to operating expenses, and $30 remained as operating income.
_Avoid_: Vertical analysis, expense distribution, cost breakdown

**Margin**:
Any profit figure shown as a percentage of revenue.
_Example_: Net income of $97 billion on revenue of $383 billion is a margin of about 25%.
_Avoid_: Profitability, markup

**Gross margin**:
Gross profit as a percentage of revenue.
_Example_: Apple's gross margin in fiscal 2023 was about 44%, while Costco's was about 12%.
_Avoid_: Gross profit margin, markup

**Operating margin**:
Operating income as a percentage of revenue.
_Example_: Apple's operating margin in fiscal 2023 was about 30%, while Costco's was about 3%.
_Avoid_: Operating profit margin, EBIT margin

**Segment**:
A part of a company whose revenue and operating income are reported separately in its filings.
_Example_: Amazon reports three segments, and its cloud segment produced about two thirds of the company's operating income in 2023.
_Avoid_: Division, business unit, business line

**Compound annual growth rate**:
The steady yearly rate that would take a number from its starting value to its ending value over several years.
_Example_: Revenue that doubles in five years grew at a compound annual growth rate of about 15%.
_Avoid_: CAGR, average growth

**Return on invested capital**:
Operating income after tax, divided by all the money put into the business by lenders and owners. It measures how well a company turns investment into profit.
_Example_: A company that earns $15 a year after tax for every $100 invested has a return on invested capital of 15%.
_Avoid_: ROIC, return on capital, ROI

**Net debt**:
Debt minus cash.
_Example_: A company with $10 billion of debt and $4 billion of cash has net debt of $6 billion.
_Avoid_: Leverage, net borrowings

### 8. The business

**Business model**:
Who pays the company, for what, and how often.
_Example_: Costco sells goods at very thin markups and earns much of its profit from annual membership fees.
_Avoid_: Strategy, revenue model

**Competitive advantage**:
The reason rivals cannot simply copy a company and take its customers.
_Example_: Shops accept Visa because nearly every shopper carries it, and shoppers carry it because nearly every shop accepts it.
_Avoid_: Moat, edge, differentiation

**Unit economics**:
What a company earns and spends on a single customer, store or item sold.
_Example_: A gym that spends $100 to sign up a member who then pays $40 a month for two years.
_Avoid_: Per-unit profitability, customer math

**Recurring revenue**:
Revenue that repeats automatically under a subscription or contract, without a new sale each time.
_Example_: In 2013 Adobe stopped releasing new versions of the design software customers paid for once and kept, and put every new version behind a subscription paid monthly or yearly.
_Avoid_: Subscription revenue, ARR, sticky revenue

### 9. What the company did

**Capital allocation**:
What a company does with its money: reinvest in the business, make acquisitions, pay down debt, pay dividends, or carry out share buybacks.
_Example_: In fiscal 2023 Apple spent about $78 billion on share buybacks and about $15 billion on dividends.
_Avoid_: Use of cash, deployment, decisions

**Acquisition**:
One company buying another.
_Example_: In 2012 Facebook agreed to buy Instagram for about $1 billion in cash and shares.
_Avoid_: Merger, takeover, deal, M&A

**Dividend**:
Money a company pays out to its shareholders, usually every quarter, as an amount per share.
_Example_: Coca-Cola has raised its dividend every year for more than 60 years.
_Avoid_: Payout, distribution

**Share buyback**:
A company using its money to buy its own shares, which leaves fewer shares and so a larger slice of the company for each one remaining.
_Example_: Through share buybacks Apple went from about 25 billion shares in 2013, counted in today's share units, to about 15 billion in 2024.
_Avoid_: Repurchase, stock buyback

**Dilution**:
The shrinking of each share's slice of the company when new shares are created.
_Example_: If a company with 100 shares creates 10 more, each old share goes from owning 1% to about 0.91%.
_Avoid_: Share issuance, watering down

**Corporate event**:
A notable thing that happened at a company that is not a capital allocation choice, such as a new chief executive, a product launch or a lawsuit.
_Example_: Satya Nadella becoming Microsoft's chief executive in February 2014.
_Avoid_: Decision, news, milestone

### 10. What was happening around it

**Economic context**:
The state of the whole economy at a point in time: interest rates, inflation, and whether there was a recession.
_Example_: During 2022 and 2023 the US faced high inflation and fast-rising interest rates at the same time.
_Avoid_: Macro, market context

**Interest rate**:
The yearly price of borrowing money, as a percentage. In the US the central bank, the Federal Reserve, sets the base level.
_Example_: The Federal Reserve raised its interest rate from near zero to above 5% during 2022 and 2023.
_Avoid_: Rates, cost of money, fed funds

**Inflation**:
The pace at which prices in general are rising, as a percentage per year.
_Example_: US inflation peaked at about 9% in June 2022.
_Avoid_: Price growth, CPI

**Recession**:
A period of several months or more when the whole economy shrinks and unemployment rises.
_Example_: The US recession that ran from December 2007 to June 2009 pushed unemployment up to a peak of 10% in October 2009.
_Avoid_: Downturn, slowdown, crash

**Industry context**:
How the company's rivals and its sector as a whole were doing at a point in time.
_Example_: In 2022 advertising spending slowed, and Meta, Alphabet and Snap all reported weaker results together.
_Avoid_: Sector trends, market context

**Index**:
A fixed list of companies whose combined share prices are tracked as one number.
_Example_: The S&P 500 is an index of about 500 of the largest US public companies.
_Avoid_: Benchmark, "the market"

**Market context**:
Whether share prices in general were rising or falling at a point in time, measured by an index.
_Example_: The S&P 500 fell about 19% in 2022.
_Avoid_: Using "market context" for the economy or the industry

**Event study**:
Measuring how a company's share price moved in the days around a specific announcement, compared with how an index moved.
_Example_: Meta's share price fell about 26% in one day in February 2022 after it reported its first-ever drop in daily users.
_Avoid_: News correlation, price reaction

### 11. What the company is worth

**Valuation**:
An estimate of what a company is worth, to compare against its market capitalization.
_Example_: Deciding that a company with a market capitalization of $50 billion is really worth about $70 billion is a valuation.
_Avoid_: Price, intrinsic value, fair value

**Enterprise value**:
Market capitalization plus net debt: what it would cost to buy the whole company and settle what it owes.
_Example_: A company with a market capitalization of $50 billion and net debt of $6 billion has an enterprise value of $56 billion.
_Avoid_: EV, firm value, takeover value

**EBITDA**:
Operating income with depreciation and amortization added back: a rough measure of the money the business produces before interest, tax and spending on long-lived things.
_Example_: Apple's EBITDA in fiscal 2023 was about $126 billion: $114 billion of operating income plus $11.5 billion of depreciation and amortization.
_Avoid_: Cash profit, operating cash flow

**Multiple**:
A company's price divided by one of its yearly figures, used to compare how expensive different companies are.
_Example_: A company with an enterprise value of $56 billion and EBITDA of $7 billion trades at a multiple of 8.
_Avoid_: Ratio, rating

**Price-to-earnings ratio**:
The share price divided by earnings per share: how many dollars buyers pay for each dollar of yearly net income.
_Example_: A share priced at $100 with earnings per share of $5 has a price-to-earnings ratio of 20.
_Avoid_: P/E, PE, earnings multiple

**Discounted cash flow**:
A valuation method that estimates all the free cash flow a company will produce in the future and works out what that is worth today.
_Example_: $100 expected ten years from now is worth about $46 today if money could otherwise earn 8% a year.
_Avoid_: DCF, cash flow model

**Margin of safety**:
The gap between a valuation and a lower share price, kept as protection in case the valuation is wrong.
_Example_: Estimating a share is worth $100 and buying only at $60 or less.
_Avoid_: Discount, cushion, upside

### 12. Collecting a company

These are the tool's own words, not words from the field.

**Collecting**:
Fetching a company's filings from the SEC and checking them, which happens only when a person asks for that company.
_Example_: Searching for Costco, finding it is not collected, and saying yes starts collecting it in the background.
_Avoid_: Pulling, importing, syncing, downloading, prefilling

**Not collected**:
The state of a company the tool knows by name and ticker only.
_Example_: Before anyone asks for it, every public company is not collected.
_Avoid_: Missing, unavailable, inactive

**Ready**:
The state of a company whose reported numbers are collected, have passed their checks, and can be opened.
_Example_: A quiet notice says "Costco is ready" while you keep reading about another company.
_Avoid_: Active, done, available, loaded

**As of**:
The date of the newest filing included in a ready company's data.
_Example_: "As of 9 October" means the newest filing the tool holds for that company was filed on 9 October.
_Avoid_: Updated, synced, the day the data was collected

**Last checked**:
The day the tool last asked the SEC whether a ready company has newer filings.
_Example_: A company can be as of 9 October and last checked today, which means nothing newer exists yet.
_Avoid_: Refreshed, updated, as of

**Calculated number**:
A figure the tool worked out from reported numbers because the company does not publish it as a single line. It is always marked as calculated.
_Example_: Free cash flow, worked out as operating cash flow minus capital expenditure.
_Avoid_: Derived, estimated, adjusted number

**Doubtful number**:
A reported number that failed one of the tool's checks. It is still shown, marked as doubtful, with the check it failed.
_Example_: A fiscal year where assets do not equal liabilities plus equity in the collected data.
_Avoid_: Error, bad data, invalid

**Gap**:
The empty place shown where a company does not report a term. It is never shown as zero.
_Example_: Costco reports no research and development, so that line is a gap.
_Avoid_: Zero, N/A, missing data

**Not covered**:
The answer the tool gives for a kind of company its terms and views do not fit, with the reason. In the first version this means banks, insurers, property trusts, and companies that do not file an annual report (10-K).
_Example_: Asking for JPMorgan explains that banks have no cost of revenue or gross profit and are not covered yet.
_Avoid_: Unsupported, blocked, error
