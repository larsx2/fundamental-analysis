# Net debt counts cash and investments

Net debt is debt minus cash and investments. Cash and investments is cash plus the bonds and shares a company could sell on a market at any time: its short-term investments, and the marketable securities it files as long-term, mostly bonds that come due more than a year later. Shares count only when the company files them as marketable securities. Stakes held as an owner or partner in other companies are left out, even when those companies are listed, because they are not held to be sold. Restricted cash is left out too, because it may be used only for a set purpose. Leases are not counted as debt. They will be shown on a line of their own.

Cash is the company's own line for cash and cash equivalents. Some companies file only a total that includes restricted cash. Then cash is that total minus the restricted cash filed for that day, taken from the total's own report or else from the latest report that gives it. When the total also includes the cash of businesses being sold, that cash is taken out too, and if it is not filed, cash is a gap. When no report gives restricted cash for that day, the total is used only if it equalled cash at the end of the nearest fiscal years before and after where both were filed. Otherwise cash is a gap.

Until now net debt was debt minus the single "cash and cash equivalents" line. Cash-rich companies keep most of their money in securities instead, so the tool showed them owing money when they held more than they owed. In the nine saved companies the sign was wrong in 12 of 27 recent fiscal years. For fiscal 2025 the tool showed Apple with $63 billion of net debt, while on its fourth-quarter earnings call Apple's finance chief reported $132 billion of cash and marketable securities against $99 billion of debt, and $34 billion of net cash. Those rounded figures give $33 billion; Apple's $34 billion comes from the unrounded ones. Microsoft, Alphabet and NVIDIA each report a total of cash and securities in their annual reports. The new definition reproduces Microsoft's for fiscal 2026 and Alphabet's for 2025. Microsoft's match holds partly because it files its longer-dated bonds in one line with its stakes in other companies, a line the tool does not read. It reproduces NVIDIA's for the years NVIDIA filed its securities under the standard names, but not for fiscal 2026, when it used a name of its own, so that year is a gap.

Practice differs at the edges. Analysts such as Wall Street Prep count marketable securities as cash. Rating agencies such as S&P subtract only the cash and liquid investments they judge accessible, and they and Aswath Damodaran add leases to debt. We follow how the companies count themselves, because a reader checks the tool against the company's own words first, and we will show leases apart so that none of it is hidden.

## Consequences

- No new data is collected: every tag in an annual report is already stored. The change is in which tags are read.
- Each company files its securities under its own choice of tag. The tool reads the first one present from short lists, and never reads a total of securities that includes cash or stakes in other companies.
- A year where a company filed its securities under a name of its own, as NVIDIA did for fiscal 2026, has a gap for cash and investments and for net debt, with the reason, rather than a smaller number.
- Until a later change adds investments, net debt still subtracts cash alone.
- Net debt can return to the Overview.
